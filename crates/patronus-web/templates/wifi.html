Wi-Fi protection

Radios

Steering

When an access point restarts, its devices move to another one and most of them never come back, even when they end up two rooms away with a weak signal. Steering finds devices that stay connected here with a weak signal and ASKS them to move (802.11v): devices that support it first say what they hear (802.11k), and are asked only when another access point of this network is clearly better; they roam without dropping the connection. A device is disconnected only as the last resort: inside the quiet hours, when it had no traffic for a while and ignored the request (or cannot be asked), at most once a day. A device that comes back just as weak is left alone. These settings apply at once, without restarting the radios.

Band steering: a device on this unit's 2.4 GHz that has shown it can use 5 GHz (it was on 5 GHz here before, or says so when it joins) is ASKED to move to this unit's 5 GHz network of the same name, where there is more room. Devices that report what they hear are asked only when they hear 5 GHz well enough. Band steering never disconnects a device; one that comes back to 2.4 GHz is left alone (twice as long each time).

Misbehaving devices

Some devices (often smart plugs far from the access point) retry joining every few seconds without ever finishing, or jump between this unit's networks. That fills the log and, on some radios, can crash the Wi-Fi. These limits make the radio ignore such a device for a while; it is let in again as soon as it really connects. Changing them restarts the radios. 0 in the first box of a row turns that limit off.

Airtime

Each device may have only a few milliseconds of airtime waiting in the radio; the rest waits in a per-device fair queue in the unit. A device that downloads at full speed, or a slow one far away, then no longer delays every other device on that radio (without it, 2.4 GHz delays reach seconds under load), and every device gets an equal share of airtime. These settings apply at once, without restarting the radios.

Channel choice

Every time the radios start, and again every night, the unit measures how busy each allowed channel is (the same short sample on every channel, its own traffic left out) and counts the other access points it hears there, then takes the best one. At night it moves only when another channel is clearly and repeatedly better, by a channel switch announcement: connected devices follow without disconnecting. Units of the same network pick one after the other and never take each other's channels. These settings apply at once, without restarting the radios.

Transmit power: "auto" lets the radio use the most the country allows; a lower value shrinks the coverage (useful when two access points overlap). Power and client names apply at once, without restarting the radios.

Channel "auto" (the default) measures how busy each allowed channel is every time the radios start and takes the least busy one; the radio cards above show the channel it picked. Pick a fixed channel only when you plan channels for several access points by hand.

Several access points with the same network names: each one measures the air itself, so two of them can end on the same channel block and share its airtime. With "keep off the channels this network's other access points use" on (the default), a unit scans for them before it starts its radios and leaves their blocks out of its automatic choice while another block remains. Later it re-checks; when another unit overlaps and has the lower MAC address, this one restarts that radio onto a free block (outside the quiet hours only when no client is connected). These settings apply at once, without restarting the radios.

Wi-Fi 6 is on by default. Turn it off only for an old client that cannot connect with it. 5 GHz channels 52-144 need a radar check (DFS) of up to ten minutes before the 5 GHz networks appear.
